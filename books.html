Math Textbook Recommendations

Since I've found it difficult to find compilations of interesting math books, here's a list of recommendations for books that I've read at least parts of and really enjoyed. They are currently in no particular order, other than how they came to mind, but I will organize it better eventually. Each book is labeled by roughly what field it discusses e.g. NT for number theory, Geometry, or Topology.

  • (Topology) A Primer on Mapping Class Groups (Farb, Margalit) - A lovely introduction to the ideas surrounding the moduli space of curves, mapping class group, and Teichmuller space which applies a lot of ideas in topology and geometry. Gives a lot of intuition behind why certain things should be true with well written explanations, multiple perspectives, and plenty of pictures.
  • (NT) An Invitation to Arithmetic Geometry (Lorenzini) - An introductory text on algebraic number theory which takes the unique stance that number theory is really geometric. The first half explains how number rings like Z[i] should really correspond to "nonsingular curves" and how their properties are reflected in this viewpoint, while the second half takes number theoretic ideas like the zeta function and analyzes them on curves over finite fields.
  • (Geometry) Algebraic Curves and Riemann Surfaces (Miranda) - Builds up the theory of complex curves analytically and topologically, and gradually transitions to how ideas like sheaves and cohomology can facilitate their study. Very good introductory text to some of the ideas of algebraic geometry that doesn't assume much background beyond complex analysis.
  • (Geometry) Visual Differential Geometry and Forms (Needham) - A very nice introduction to differential geometry that gives intuition for and explains what ideas like a metric and curvature actually mean. Very well written with plenty of pictures, clever arguments, and historical anecdotes.
  • (Topology) Gauge Fields, Knots, and Gravity (Baez, Munian) - Builds up many topological ideas such as differential forms, gauge theory, and curvature with motivation from both math and physics. The latter part of the book gives a high-level introduction to very deep modern ideas on how gauge theory can be used to construct many classical knot invariants. The writing flows very well and reads more like a story than a textbook.
  • (Topology) The Geometry of Four Manifolds (Donaldson, Kronheimer) - Utilizes various tools from all over topology to build up to Donaldson's Fields Medal work of using gauge theory to construct invariants of differential four manifolds. It takes the scenic route to its destination and along the way explains many ways in which the different facets of topology (complex geometry, vector bundles, analytic aspects, cohomology, ...) all interplay with each other very well in four dimensions. A bit heavy on prerequisites, but very worthwhile.
  • (Topology) Stable Homotopy and Generalized Homology (Switzer) - A very nice follow up to a first course in algebraic geometry which explains the notions of homotopy theory starting with higher homotopy groups and building towards how homology can be seen as a special case of homotopy via the theory of spectra.
  • (Topology) The Wild World of Four Manifolds (Scorpan) - A very nice introduction to many ideas and examples in four dimensional topology pulling from many different parts of topology. Rather than give a rigorous treatment of everything, it aims to provide intuition through plenty of explanation and pictures. Very good book to read to familiarize yourself with topology beyond surfaces and really feel what topology is all about.
  • (Topology) Differential Forms in Algebraic Topology (Bott, Tu) - A very nice introduction to the notions of cohomology to understand manifolds through the analytic view of differential forms. Has many concrete constructions and computations with cohomology which is often glossed over in more abstract treatments.
  • (Topology) Morse Theory (Milnor) - A relatively short but very nicely written text on how to use functions to study the topology of a space, building up to a proof of the famed Bott periodicity theorem.
  • (Lie Groups) Lie Groups Applied to Differential Equations (Olver) - A perspective on Lie groups closer to the original motivation of using them to analyze symmetries of differential equations and their solutions. Builds up the theory with this motivation with many interesting computations and plenty of physical and mathematical applications.
  • (Geometry) The Laplacian on a Riemannian Manifold (Rosenberg) - A nice introduction to how the concepts of the laplacian and heat kernel on manifolds can be used to analytically prove many results in topology and geometry. Motivates the necessary theory of Riemannian geometry along the way.
  • (Topology) Spin Geometry (Lawson, Michelsohn) - Introduces the ideas of spin structures and index theorems building up to the Atiyah-Singer index theorem. The first section is an excellent introduction to Clifford algebras which actually works out what they are explicitly. Then there is a nice discussion of spin structures on manifolds and various differential operators and index theorems that come from them. The discussion on K theory is particularly nice because it introduces ways to work with it analogous to ordinary cohomology theory e.g. relative K theory and orientation classes.
  • (Algebraic Geometry) Principles of Algebraic Geometry (Griffiths, Harris) - In my opinion, the best introduction to algebraic geometry for someone more topologically minded. Chapter 0 is very difficult as it has a lot of ideas, but if you can understand it, the rest of the book is a pleasure to go through. Uses the language of line bundles and cohomology that will make you sound smart, but rather than work in abstractions of schemes, it works very concretely in the realm of manifolds with plenty of examples (I was surprised as to how much could be proven by different ways of looking at curvature). It only focuses on complex manifolds and doesn't cover all the foundations, but is a good place to get groundings to read more abstract books like Hartshorne.
  • (Topology) Topology and Analysis: The Atiyah-Singer Index Formula and Gauge Theoretic Physics (Booss, Bleecker) - Bit of an old fashioned typescript, but the pictures and abundance of prose more than make up for it. The first section covers the topology of K theory in relation with Fredholm operators while the second section introduces the analysis of elliptic PDEs and pseudodifferential operators, coming together in a discussion of the Atiyah-Singer index formula in the third part. Overall very readable as the mathematical theorems are supplemented with plenty of examples and explanations for the ideas of proofs, historical context, and why one should care about these ideas.
  • (Algebraic Geometry) An Introduction to Invariants and Moduli (Mukai) - Very well written book on some things you can do with algebraic geometry. In particular, the book discusses quotients by algebraic groups and explains the ideas behind geometric invariant theory. Has many nice applications including numerical examples of Hilbert series near the beginning, Riemann-Roch, Jacobians and moduli spaces of vector bundles, and more. Definitely difficult if you aren't comforable with basic ideas of algebraic geometry, but if you've taken a class in it and are wondering "what can you do with all this," I think this is a good book.
  • (Physics) Quantum Field Theory (Ryder) - Maybe it's the good writing style, or it was just the 5th time I've tried learning QFT, but this book has been one of the better expositions on the subject for me. The book points out small details which really help with understanding the symbolism - e.g. the reason for commutation relations is that known physical equations are linear, so they impose a vector space, but not an algebra structure, so we are free to impose a noncommutative one, and also a good discussion of sources in the path integral. Also has some discussion of mathematical topics like connections and curvature which is often absent from intro QFT books.
  • (Physics) Condensed Matter Field Theory (Altland, Simons) - Nice perspective on how some ideas of QFT can be used to answer some problems in statistical mechanics. Nicely explained examples give an enjoyable read.
  • (NT) Algebraic Number Theory (Cassels, Frohlich) - Classic reference on class field theory; definitely not for a first read, but a nice book to solidify ideas.
  • (NT) Modular Functions and Dirichlet Series in Number Theory (Apostol) - Very well written and elementary (only needing some complex analysis) book on modular forms and related ideas. It starts with a beautifully written exposition of Weierstrass p function and how it relates to Eisenstein series. It proceeds with topics like Hecke operators and a proof (modulo some referenced theorems) of Rademacher's explicit formula for the partition function.
  • (NT) Primes of the Form x2+ny2 (Cox) - A book that promises a lot. It has 3 parts with increasing content with corresponding increasing required background. It starts with Gauss's classical theory of quadratic forms, and doesn't need much more than group theory. It follows with the theory of quadratic forms from the point of view of algebraic number theory and class field theory, and finishes with explicit examples needing some theory of Elliptic curves. It's not an easy book and has taken me a few rereads, but is particularly nice in elucidating abstract theory into something concrete. The first part nicely describes how to compute class groups of quadratic extensions of Q, the second gives nice insights into class field theory, and the third part gains insights into algebraic NT via special functions like the p function and modular forms, along with many beautiful computations.
  • (NT) An Introduction to Cyclotomic Fields (Washington) - So far, I've only read the first few sections of the book, but it has a lot to offer. After some introduction to facts about cyclotomic fields, it proves that p divides the class number of Q(zeta_p) if and only if p divides the numerator of some Bernoulli number! It does this through the use of p-adic L-functions. I didn't understand p-adic analysis too much, so I first looked at Serre's theory of p-adic modular forms in relation to the Kummer congruences (not discussed in the book, but it gives a potentially easier place where p-adic analysis can be used to deduce divisibility). Later on, the book dicusses Iwasawa theory, which is a way to fully understand the p part of the class group of Q(zeta_p^n), which again, has deep ties to these p-adic L-functions.
  • (Geometry/Analysis) Geometric Measure Theory: A Beginner's Guide (Morgan) - The book gives an overview on geometric measure theory, which are some methods on understanding minimal surfaces without constraint on the topology, which leads to analysis of things like soap bubbles. Very well written book which gets across the primary ideas through many pictures. Difficult proofs are often sketched with the main ideas so you can tell what's needed without getting lost in details, but when proofs are doable or important, they are provided. I think more books should be written like this to give a gentle but informative overview of a subject.
  • (Lie Groups) Lectures on Lie Groups (Adams) - A classic book outlining the classification theory of Lie gorups from a topological and invariant perspective. This should not be your first introduction to Lie groups, but if you're deep enough that you keep seeing "root systems" and you want to want to know what they are (and you're topologically minded), this is the book for you! Includes some very nice perspectives such as treating real and quaternionic reps along with the complex ones and discussing Stiefel diagrams and how to read off the fundamental group of a Lie group from it.
  • (NT) A Classical Introduction to Modern Number Theory (Ireland, Rosen) - Lately, I've been a bit disillusioned by abstract theory, and am appreciating more explicit computations. Ireland, Rosen is a great book that gives plenty of explicit computations as applications of the developed theory. Admittedly, I'm able to enjoy this much more after gaining a proper understanding of quadratic reciprocity from PROMYS, but is a very digestible book nonetheless.
  • (NT) A First Course on Modular Forms (Diamond, Shurman) - Despite the name, I don't think this should be your first exposure to modular forms, but it is a great read once you're somewhat acquainted. The book discusses the ideas around modularity - the theorem who's proof led to the proof of Fermat's last theorem. It discusses many equivalent formulations and the relationships between them, with the first being that every elliptic curve with rational j-invariant is the holomorphic image of a modular curve, and the last one is the popular statement that point counts of such an elliptic curve over finite fields is reflected in a weight 2 modular form. The book also ends with some explanation of how Galois representations enter the picture. What I like about the book is it doesn't shy away from using different techniques that fit best: geometric (cotangent bundle, Riemann-Roch), analytic (many explicit examples of constructions of modualar forms), algebraic (algebraic manipulation of the Hecke algebra), etc. It builds a very coherent story, and each chapter is a pleasure in itself.
  • (Physics) Quantum Field Theory in a Nutshell (Zee) - The other books I've listed on Quantum Field Theory are good, but I think this one wins on top, because it's the only one that explains at a conceptual level how to use the computations in QFT. For example, I never understood how to use the partition function, but this book very early explains that Z[J]~e^iEt and uses it to find the energy of a system, and thus (by modifying J), shows what effective forces are caused by photons and gravitons, deriving the inverse square law. As you proceed through the book, it has many gems after the computational introduction: anomalies, kinks/vortices/monopoles/instantons, fractional quantum hall effect, wilson loops, and on and on. It doesn't go into great detail into any one topic, but is an excellent panorama of ideas and applications.
  • (NT) Pi and the AGM (Borwein, Borwein) - Take two positive real numbers a,b; take their arithmetic and geometric mean; take the arithmetic and geometric mean of these two, and continue infinitely. The AM-GM inequalities imply this will converge to a number called the AGM mean of a,b. It turns out, if a=1, b=x, then the AGM is essentially an elliptic integral of x. It also turns out that it's some function of theta series of x. Actually, the convergence is very fast - roughly doubling the digits of accuracy with each iteration. This book is full of such gems and uses the manipulations to obtain recursive and explicit formulas for these functions and π, including Ramanujan's famous formula which gets 8 digits on each iteration. Along the way, many beautiful topics are touched upon including modular forms and the Rogers-Ramanujan identities. Very pleasurable for-fun read.
  • (Topology) Notes on Cobordism Theory (Stong) - Great read for the ideas of how to use the ideas of homotopy theory to compute cobordism groups.
  • (Topology) Stable Homotopy and Generalized Homology (Adams) - A good book for the overview of the ideas behind stable homotopy theory and the introductory theory of spectra.
  • (Topology) Cohomology Operations and Applications in Homotopy Theory (Mosher, Tangora) - A very good introduction to the mod 2 Steenrod Squares and how to be able to actually do some computations in homotopy theory with the Serre spectral sequence and other tools. Ends up with the computation of the first few stable stems and proving that they are generated by the various Hopf fibrations.
  • (NT/Geometry) Hilbert Modular Forms (Freitag) - Discusses the theory of Hilbert Modular Forms: generalizations of classical modular forms from an action of SL2(OK) for K a totally real algebraic number field on a product of hyperbolic planes via the different embeddings into R. Discusses some basic constructions of Poincare and Eisenstein series, derives the dimension of such modular forms through analytic (Selberg trace formula) and algebro-geometric means. Also uses these tools to compute the cohomology groups of the quotient variety.
  • (Topology) Classifying Spaces for Surgery and Cobordism of Manifolds (Madsen, Milgram) - A melting pot for various beautiful ideas in topology. For example: an overview of how to determine if a homotopy type admits a manifold, how to understand the topology of various classifying spaces like BG which classifies stable spherical fibrations, and using localization in topology to relate various classifying spaces.
  • (Geometry) The Seiberg Witten Equations and Applications to the Topology of Smooth Four-Manifolds (Morgan) - A very nice discussion of the gauge theory of the Seiberg-Witten equations, which is much more nicely behaved than Donaldson theory. Discusses analytic details behind the construction of the moduli spaces, and defines the invariants that come with them.
  • (Geometry) Geometric Analysis and Applications to Quantum Field Theory (Bouwknegt, Wu) - An excellent discussion of various aspects of geometry that have arisen from physics such as magnetic monopoles, some aspects of Chern-Simons theory, and the best explanation for the origin of the Seiberg-Witten equations that I have found so far.
  • (NT) Number Theory 2: Introduction to Class Field Theory (Kato, Kurokawa, Saito) - A very nice discussion of class field theory and zeta functions. Their proof of class field theory proceeds through the theory of division algebras, which is hard to find elsewhere and is also nice in that it shows a connection between the local and global theory (and avoids Galois cohomology).
  • (NT) Number Theory 3: Iwasawa Theory and Modular Forms (Kurokawa, Kurihara, Saito) - Great elementary introduction to the theory of modular forms, and also a very nice discussion of Iwasawa theory. They discuss the original motivation that Iwasawa had for looking for a relationship between class numbers of cyclotomic fields and L-functions and discusses the proof of the main theorem relating special values of the zeta functions with class groups using p-adic analysis and the Iwasawa algebra.
  • (NT) An Introduction to p-adic L-functions (Jacinto, Williams) - A discussion of the ideas of p-adic zeta functions and Iwasawa theory. Gives a great introduction to the construction of p-adic L-functions via measure theory and the various ways to understand the space of p-adic distributions including the Iwasawa algebra. Later discusses Iwasawa theory and a proof of the Iwasawa theorem.
